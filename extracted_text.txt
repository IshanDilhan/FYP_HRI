Abstract
Human Robot Interaction is shifting from the command-based control systems to
more complex systems. This is because the command-based systems are natu-
ral and intelligent communication. For robots to be able to successfully interact
with humen, they have to listen not only to spoken directions but also to hu-
man emotions, actions, and the surrounding environment. This project illustrates
an adaptive Human Robot Interaction framework that allows robots to respond
intelligently by learning from multimodal human cues.
The proposed system integrates emotion recognition, gesture and motion un-
derstanding, and context awareness using advanced techniques from Artificial In-
telligence and Computer Vision. Unlike traditional rule-based systems, this model
dynamically weights cues based on their reliability and the surrounding seman-
tic context, allowing it to resolve scenarios where human cues are conflicting or
ambiguous also.
By combining multimodal perception with context aware policy learning, the
project contributes toward building ösocially awareö robots that can interact with
humans in a more empathetic, flexible and meaningful way. This approach aims
to enhance the naturalness, safety and effectiveness of human robot collaboration
in real world environments.

Chapter 1
Introduction
Modern service robotics is currently grappling with a phenomenon known asBe-
havioral Rigidity, where machines fail to adapt their physical actions to the
nuanced emotional and contextual signals inherent in human communication. Cur-
rent systems are capable of detecting discrete human gestures, such as ôpointingö
at an exit, but it often fails to identify the meaning or the underlying intent of
that gesture. For example, whether that ôpointingö at an exit action is a helpful
request for directions or an angry complaint regarding service. To address this
problem, this project proposes aReal-Time Multimodal Intent-Synthesis
Framework, followed byintent and context-aware policy learning. By
fusing four different pillars of human communication, such as emotion, motion,
gesture, and context, this system aims to resolve conflicting social cues and pro-
vide robotic responses that are not only physically safe but also socially aware and
proactive.
1.1 Background
The evolution of human-robot interaction (HRI) has gone beyond simply perform-
ing a task. Robots should be able to interpret the öwhyö behind human actions.
This helps robots to behave proactively rather than reactively. This evolution is
driven by several key factors:
òDominance of Non-Verbal Communication:Effective human commu-
nication heavily relies on a complex non-verbal signal, which includes facial
micro-expressions, body language, and hand gestures. Academic research
suggests that a significant portion of emotional intent is conveyed through
these non-verbal channels, making social vision a prerequisite for empathetic
robotics [1].
òChallenge of Semantic Ambiguity:A core problem in social robotics
is semantic ambiguity, where a single gesture carries different meanings de-
pending on the userÆs emotional state and physical environment. For exam-
ple, a person running towards a robot with a smile may symbolize a greet-
ing, while the same movement with a frightened expression may indicate an
emergency.
1

--- PAGE 8 ---
òTechnological Shift toward Transformers:Traditional rule-based (IFûTHEN)
systems are considered too rigid to handle the ambiguous and conflicting na-
ture of social data. Modern architectures are moving toward Transformer-
based models. Transformer-based models utilize self-attention mechanisms
to weigh different input streams simultaneously to resolve social dissonance.
òHardware and Edge-Compute Advancements:The migration of com-
plex ôsocial brainsö from remote servers to internal robotic platforms is en-
abled by advances in edge-computing hardware. Platforms like the NVIDIA
Jetson Orin Nano, combined with optimization tools like TensorRT, allow
for high-frequency processing. This is necessary for secure and responsive
real-time interactions.
1.2 Problem Statement
Although there have been significant advances in individual sensing technologies,
current HRI systems remain emotionally blind and behaviorally rigid. Most ex-
isting frameworks rely on fixed and rule-based policies, which fail to capture the
dynamic nature of human behavior. This creates several critical challenges:
òAmbiguity of Single Modalities:A major limitation of current systems
is their inability to resolve ambiguity when relying on a single source of
information. For instance, a raised-hand gesture may represent a greeting, a
command to stop, or an expression of surprise, depending on the userÆs facial
emotion and environmental context. Without multimodal fusion, robots
frequently misinterpret human intent.
òInadequate Multimodal Fusion:Existing systems often process emo-
tions, gestures, and contextual cues as single inputs rather than as a fused
signal. This leads to fragmented perception and a lack of holistic under-
standing.
òRigid Interaction Models:Rule-based models often fail to adapt to hu-
man behavior in real time with varying human emotions or environmental
contexts. This leads to unnatural or frustrating interactions between humans
and robots.
Therefore, there is a clear need for a lightweight, multimedia HRI framework
that jointly perceives emotional, gestural, and contextual cues to dynamically and
efficiently adapt robot responses.

òSynthesize a High-Fidelity Multimodal Datasetû To generate a high-
quality dataset comprising over 60 socially ambiguous interaction scenarios
using NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Audio2Face, with automatic ground-truth an-

òOptimize for Edge Deploymentû To deploy and optimize the trained

Scope
This project focuses on the end-to-end design, integration, and deployment of a
complete HRI system that enables socially intelligent robotic behavior. A key
challenge addressed in this work is the lack of a unified dataset that jointly rep-
resents context, emotion, gesture, and motion, which are essential components of
socially aware robot decision-making.
To address this limitation, the project emphasizes dataset integration and syn-
thetic data generation, supported by self-supervised learning techniques, to bridge
gaps between modality-specific datasets and enable coherent multimodal learning.
What is IN Scope:
òEnvironments:The study is limited to indoor service settings (specifically
Hospitals, Museums, and Retail areas) where social norms are well-defined.
òHuman Cues:The system will focus on non-verbal communication only.
This includes facial micro-expressions (Affect), body language (Motion), and
hand signals (Gesture).
òInteraction Type:One-on-one interactions between a single human and a
single robot.
What is OUT of Scope:
òVerbal Communication:The project will not involve NLP or speech-to-
text, focusing purely on visual/physical ôSocial Vision.ö
òCrowd Dynamics:The model is not intended for ôSocial Navigationö in
dense crowds (e.g., a busy train station); it focuses on direct interpersonal
intent.
òOutdoor Operation:Weather-related variables, uneven terrain, and out-
door lighting fluctuations are excluded.
òMechanical Design:The project uses off-the-shelf mobile bases (AgileX
LIMO); it does not involve the design or fabrication of new robotic hardware.

Chapter 2
Literature Review
2.1 Previous Work
2.1.1 HRI with rule-based systems
Traditional HRI systems mostly rely on rigid, rule-based scripts to govern robot
behavior. Therefore these systems typically use pre programmed öif-thenö logic
to map specific inputs to the outputs such asöif the user is happy, offer to play a
gameö [2]. However, this approach lacks the flexibility required for real world social
interaction. Mamodiyaetal. (2025) [3] note that these systems depend mainly on
predefined scripts or single perceptual modalities. It makes themöbrittleöwhen fa-
cial cues are occluded or lighting varies [4]. Furthermore, AhmedandAleem(2025)
[5] shows that, as these serobots are preprogrammed for specifictasks, they fail
to recognize human emotions or communicate with contextual cues. It prevents
them from adapting to changing environments.
2.1.2 HRI with single modality
Many existing HRI systems use single input type, which often leads to incomplete
perceptions of the userÆs state.
òGestures Only: Systems focus on gestures often struggle with environmental
interference. As a example, the geometric relationship methods for the ges-
ture recognition fail in noisy environments or complex backgrounds [6]. Ac-
cording to the subject differences in performing the same gesture like speed
or range of motion, make it difficult for single-modality models to learn un-
seen feature variations to recognition failure [7]. But these systems have not
ability to interpret intent or handle ambiguity without corroborating data.
òEmotion Only: Research relying only on emotion recognition faces signif-
icant robustness issues. Bamforth(2024) [8] notes that face based systems
incorrect or fail when faces are blurred, not in the frame, or in low light. Lin
et al. (2023) [9] further argue that main stream emotion recognition methods
focus on short-term perception, disregarding the context in which emotions
are perceived ,which leads to identification failures. But these systems lack
context and robustness against occlusion.
4

òMotion Only: Systems using only motion sensors or vision-based motion
extraction can capture physical movement but miss the semantic meaning
behind it. While effective for basic signaling, utilizing sensors can introduce
increased communication costs and complexities, as individuals are required
to wear sensor sets [10]. But motion data alone fails to capture the öwhyö
behind an action (intent) or the emotional state of the user.
2.1.3 HRI with fusing more than one modality
To overcome single-modality limitations, recent research has focused on fusing
multiple inputs.
òECRP (Mamodiya et al., 2025) [3]: This framework fuses facial and vocal
emotion cues using a confidence-weighted attention layer. It integrates these
with a context vector (history, time-of-day) into a DDQN for adaptive task
planning [11].
òTransformer-Based Fusion (Mohamed et al.) [12]: This approach utilizes
modality-specific encoders for facial thermal data, facial AUs, and textual
context. These are combined via additive fusion and processed by a shared
transformer encoder to capture temporal dependencies.
òFAM-HRI (Lai et al., 2025) [13]: This system integrates gaze and speech
data captured by wearable ARIA glasses. It uses a GPT-based agent to
align spoken commands with gaze fixation periods to determine user intent
in cluttered environments [14].
òContext-Aware Two-Stream Network (Wang et al., 2024) [10]: This method
fuses first-person vision (video) with motion sensory data. One stream ex-
tracts visual context using attention maps (gaze), while the second processes
motion data, combining them to recognize gestures for construction robots.
òHierarchical Fusion Engine (Ahmed Aleem, 2025) [5]: This framework pro-
cesses visual (facial), auditory (speech tone), and kinesthetic inputs. It uses
a hierarchical fusion engine to integrate emotional and environmental data
at feature, semantic, and decision levels to drive a collaborative decision
engine.
2.1.4 HRI on edge devices
Deploying multimodal models on edge devices is critical for real-time interac-
tion but presents computational challenges. Park et al. (2025) [15] utilized the
NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin to optimize open-vocabulary perception pipelines, us-
ing TensorRT to bridge the öreal-time gapö and balancing accuracy with latency.
Mamodiya et al. (2025) [3] optimized their ECRP framework for the NVIDIA
Jetson Nano. They demonstrated that while Raspberry Pi 4 struggled (4 fps), the
Jetson Nano with TensorRT acceleration achieved 30 fps and sub-400ms latency,
proving the feasibility of complex fusion on low-power hardware [16].

2.1.5 Available datasets for HRI research
Data is essential for training adaptive HRI systems. The FER+ [3] and CK+
[8] datasets are widely used for facial emotion training, while RAVDESS [3]
and IEMOCAP [9] are standard for vocal and multimodal emotion recognition.
Chalearn is a large-scale RGB-D dataset for gesture recognition, containing both
isolated and continuous gestures performed by multiple subjects. Widely used for
vision-based hand/arm gesture recognition. For motion and action recognition,
the NTU RGB-D [17] dataset is a common benchmark. To address the scarcity
of real-world interaction data, simulations are frequently used. Mamodiya et al.
(2025) [3] utilized a ROS-Gazebo environment to pre-train their reinforcement
learning policy before fine-tuning it on real hardware.
2.2 Gaps in Literature
2.2.1 Lack of ambiguous social data
A significant gap that can be seen is the scarcity of unified datasets that cap-
ture all interaction pillars simultaneously. Most of the existing datasets focus
more on pairs of modalities such as Audio-Visual rather than the full spectrum
of Emotion, Gesture, Motion, and Context. Lin et al. (2023) [8] shows that the
emotion recognition becomes more complex when öinformation conflicts arise be-
tween modalities,ö yet few datasets exist to train robots to resolve these conflicts
effectively.
2.2.2 Fragmentation and Temporal Blindness in Intent Syn-
thesis
Current research treats multimodal fusion as a secondary task which leads to
temporal misalignment. Lai et al. (2025) [13] point out that only few methods
model the ötemporal alignment between gaze and speech,ö which note that the
natural gaze behavior is noisy and users may look at an object moments before
speaking. Viswanath et al. (2025) [18] argues that the robots often receive verbal
commands (e.g., öput the cup thereö) that do not align perfectly in time with their
visual perception. It suggest necessity of having a dedicated öTemporal Alignment
moduleö to evaluate the relevance of inputs.
2.2.3 Computational Bottleneck on Edge Robotics Plat-
forms
There is a difference between high accurate AI models and the mobile robot hard-
ware capabilities. Park et al. (2025) [15] highlight this öreal-time gap,ö note that
while VLMs do the powerful recognition. Their high computational cost makes
them difficult to deploy on edge devices like the Jetson Orin without optimization.
This bottleneck forces a trade between semantic understanding and reaction speed
[19].

their behavior is a reason to öBehavioral Rigidityö. Existing approaches often do

in robots that can sense a user is frustrated but cannot change dynamically their
task planning to clear that frustration.

3.1.1 Multimodal Feature Extraction
Four independent models are trained separately to capture key aspects of human
behavior and environmental awareness. These models focus on human emotion
recognition, human gesture recognition, human movement understanding, and in-
teraction context identification. Training each model independently allows precise
learning of each cue before higher-level fusion.
The raw image stream is processed by the robot using perception models that can
work in real-time. Facial expression analysis is employed for extracting emotional
expressions, while skeletal and hand pose models are used for gesture recognition.
Human motion is analyzed by determining velocity and direction between frames,
while environmental markers are detected for interaction context.
All the extracted cues are then converted to numerical feature vectors for angles,
distances, intensity of movement, and spatial relations. Such a representation
makes it easier to process visual data as it reduces complexities in visual data.
3.1.2 Cross-Modal Fusion Architecture
This is the heart of the process of understanding the intent, where all the four
cues: human emotion, gesture, movement, and interaction context, are combined
to understand what the user actually wants to do. All the cues are not considered
to be of equal importance at all times. Rather, the system considers all the cues
at once and gives more importance to those cues which best identify the current
situation.
This fusion makes it possible for the system to cope with cases where human
behavior is ambiguous or mixed. For example, a person can display a behavior
of engagement through a gesture, but when he or she moves rapidly, it shows
urgency. In this case, movement-related information will become more significant
than gestures for determining the purpose.
Intent explains the reason behind a personÆs actions. While the perception stage
identifies visible behaviors such as facial expressions, gestures, and movement, the
intent layer focuses on understanding why those actions are happening and what
is likely to happen next. This allows the robot to respond in advance instead of
reacting only after a problem occurs.
Compared to rule based approaches, which rely on fixed conditions and respond
only when a rule is triggered, intent based decision making allows the robot to
behave more naturally and safely in real world human interactions. For example,
When the emotion is angry and gesture is wave , due to their conflicting nature
rule-based system gives an error while four-cues fused model identified it as frus-
trated or help seeking request from human. Also when the emotion is panicked

The Limitation of Rule-Based Systems in Social Robotics
In this subsection we explain some of the main limitations of rule-based systems
and how our proposed fusion method will help to mitigate those limitations by
using some practical scenarios.
1.The Complexity of Fuzzy Social Cues
Human social behavior is often unclear and strongly dependent
on context. Emotions, gestures, movements, and situations rarely
convey meaning on their own. Rule-based systems rely on fixed
IFûTHEN logic, which makes them poorly suited to handle such
uncertainty.
The same action can represent different intentions depending on
accompanying cues. For example, a smile with a hand wave may
indicate a greeting, while a smile combined with fast movement
may indicate urgency or stress. Handling these variations would
require a large and complex set of rules, which becomes difficult
to manage as the number of cues increases.
The Fusion of Four Cues approach addresses this limitation by
jointly considering emotion, gesture, movement, and context. In-
stead of relying on predefined rules, the system evaluates all cues
together to determine the most likely intent, allowing it to handle
ambiguous social situations more effectively.
2.Handling Dissonant (Conflicting) Data
Humans frequently often give conflicting signals, and social behav-
ior is not very easy to read. A rule-based strategy tends to stumble
here, occasionally missing one cue or misreading how cues fit to-
gether. Take a grin combined with rapid movements-that can trip
up a rigid rule system. The Fusion of Four Cues model repairs
this by taking all emotion, gesture, movement, and context into
consideration in concert. It does so by evaluating such factors in
a manner that can identify the actual patterns-things like distin-
guishing an ôAngry Waveö from a ôHappy Wave.ö
3.Temporal Dependencies
Human intention is not something static; itÆs a process that takes
time. Rule-governance systems fail to capture that, since their
